Playing fair

Lockwright is a game about an emergency locksmith who helps people. Just like in the real trade, we check first that someone has the right to enter, and only then do we open the door.

First: is this really your door?

Before every lock there is a short scene at the door. The customer lays one, two or three cards on the table: an ID, a lease, a letter with the address, a neighbour who knows them. The player can ask for one more thing. The cards are symbolic — the game never shows what a real document looks like or how to "check" one technically. What counts is common sense: does the customer's story hold together?

Sometimes the answer is no

Some jobs exist to say "no": someone wants to get into an ex-partner's flat, someone else offers double the rate "without the procedures". In those scenes the only good choice is a calm refusal — or a call to the family or the police. The game rewards refusing with reputation; it never punishes it. The police in the game are ordinary and polite; there are no getaways or chases.

A game, not a course

Every lock, padlock and safe in the game is invented. They carry no brands and depict no real products, and their proportions were changed on purpose so you can read them (the shear-line gap, for example, is several times wider than in a real cylinder). The game teaches you to feel a mechanism, not to open real doors. If you're locked out, call a locksmith or your building manager.

No burglar aesthetic

There are no balaclavas, no loot, no night-time jobs on the sly in Lockwright. There's a stairwell in warm light, a workshop, a cat in the bathroom and soup on the stove. The locksmith leaves when the door is open without a scratch and the customer is home.

Fair to the player

  • No ads, no energy, no buying coins and no randomised rewards. You earn your tools by working.
  • The game works offline and collects no data (details: privacy policy).
  • Haptics are never the only signal: everything you feel under your thumb can also be heard and seen.
